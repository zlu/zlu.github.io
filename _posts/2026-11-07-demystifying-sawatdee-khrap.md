That photo is fourteen years old. I went for yoga. I left loving Thailand, its people, food, and culture. Over the years, I kept going back. But I have never learned enough to read a simple road sign. I am finally tired of that. This blog is my foray into learning Thai, starting with the first word I should have been able to decode years ago.

**สวัสดีครับ**

I looked at a few Thai language books. Overwhelming. Consonant charts, tone rules, phrase lists, then a wall of script with almost no explanation of *why* a letter sounds one way here and another way there. None of them decoded a single everyday word the way I needed: letter by letter, sound by sound, plus the history that makes the spelling stop looking arbitrary.

So I am writing that kind of note for myself, and folding some of it into [NidNoi](https://nidnoi.dev). If it helps someone else, good. The method is simple: pick a real word I already hear, take it apart until the reading rule is forced into the open, then ask why Thai kept that spelling at all.

This post is my first wall: why **สวัสดี** looks denser than “hello,” where the word comes from, and why the second **ส** (the same letter that hissed at the start) suddenly sounds like a soft **t**.

## The phrase, before I take it apart

| Thai | Rough sound | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| สวัสดี | sa-wat-dee | hello / well-being |
| ครับ | khrap | polite particle (male speakers) |
| ค่ะ | kha | polite particle (female speakers) |

So **สวัสดีครับ / สวัสดีค่ะ** is “hello,” said politely. The greeting is **สวัสดี**. The **ครับ / ค่ะ** is the soft landing at the end: politeness that matches the *speaker*, not the listener. I use **ครับ**.

## Letter by letter: สวัสดี → sa-wat-dee

Thai does not space inside a word the way English does, but the sound still walks in beats.

| Piece | Sound | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ส | /s/ | first ส · syllable start |
| วั | wa | ว + **ั**, short *a* above ว |
| ส | **/t/** | second ส |
| ดี | dee | ด + ี |

Put together: **sa → wat → dee**.

Same letter **ส**. Two different sounds, which is the trick that no phrasebook bothered to spell out:

- At the **start** of a syllable → /s/ (the first ส)
- At the **end** of a syllable → unreleased /t/ (the second ส)

In **สวัสดี**, the second ส closes the first syllable. Tongue touches for *t*. No popped English “t.” No hissing /s/ at the end.

Thai has a family of ending-sound rules: many consonants change what they do when they sit at the back of a syllable. Day one for me: **final ส → /t/**

But why did Thai bother to write an **ส** there at all? If the sound is /t/, why not just write a **ต** or **ด**?

That question is where the word stopped being a spelling puzzle for me and turned into a little history of the Thai language.

## Where สวัสดี comes from (and why the second ส is still an ส)

**สวัสดี** did not begin life as ordinary “hello.”

It comes from Sanskrit **svasti**: a blessing that means well-being, welfare, *may you be well*.

Words like this came into Thai with Indian learning and Buddhism. For centuries Thai kept borrowing and adapting Sanskrit and Pali, especially for polite, learned, and ceremonial speech. The writing system itself is part of that inheritance: Thai letters are cousins of Indic scripts, and they are good at keeping the look of a borrowed word even when the sound changed.

So when Thai took in **svasti**, it did not just invent a casual street greeting. It kept a spelling that still points back to the Sanskrit form, including those **s** letters.

For a long time, people greeted more with the **wai** (palms together) than with this spoken word. The hands were often the hello; the fixed phrase caught up later.

Phraya Upakit Silapasan (พระยาอุปกิตศิลปสาร) is credited with adapting **สวัสดี** as a greeting at Chulalongkorn University in the 1930s, and on **22 January 1943** (พ.ศ. 2486) the government of Field Marshal Plaek Phibunsongkhram promoted it as the official spoken hello for public life. Summary with further Thai-language citations: [Thai Wikipedia, สวัสดี](https://th.wikipedia.org/wiki/สวัสดี). An old wish for someone’s well-being, folded into *sa-wat-dee*.

### Why the second ส sounds like /t/

Thai spelling is often **etymological**. It likes to keep a footprint of where a word came from. Sanskrit had an *s* in that spot; Thai kept writing **ส**.

But Thai **speech** has strict rules about what can live at the end of a syllable. Spoken Thai does not happily end a syllable with a hissing /s/. Final consonants collapse into a small set of allowed endings. An **ส** that closes a syllable joins the family that is heard as an unreleased **/t/**.

## Add ครับ / ค่ะ: the polite ending

- **ครับ** (*khrap*): male speakers
- **ค่ะ** (*kha*): female speakers

It marks politeness on the speaker’s side.

Another ending rule in **ครับ**: final **บ** is spoken as unreleased **/p/**, not a voiced English “b.” Same family of ending-sound changes as final **ส → /t/** in **สวัสดี**.

**สวัสดีครับ.** Hello, and may you be well.
